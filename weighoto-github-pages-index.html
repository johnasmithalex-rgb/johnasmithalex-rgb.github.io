Weighoto is a small UK team writing about weight loss in plain English. No crash diets, no miracle products — just honest guides on eating well, moving more, sleeping better, and building habits that actually stick. We write for real life: busy weeks, family meals, GP appointments, and scales that never seem to agree with anyone.

Start with food, not punishment

Most weight-loss advice starts by taking things away. A better start is to add things first: a proper breakfast instead of skipping it, vegetables on the plate you already eat, and water through the day. When your meals are built around protein, fibre, and foods that actually fill you up, the snacks and second helpings often sort themselves out — not by willpower, but because you are no longer starving by 4pm.

The British Dietetic Association makes the same point in its own guidance: the diets that work are the ones you can live with. A plan that bans your favourite foods usually ends the same way — a perfect week, then a bad weekend, then giving up entirely. Smaller changes last longer. Swap one sugary drink for water. Cook one extra meal at home each week. Add a portion of vegetables to dinner. None of these feels like a diet, and that is exactly why they work.

Move a little, every day

You do not need a gym membership to start moving more. A 20-minute walk after lunch, taking the stairs, or a short stretch before bed all count. The goal is not to burn a magic number of calories in one session. It is to make movement a normal part of your day, so it keeps happening in week six as well as week one.

The NHS Active 10 programme is built on this exact idea: just 10 minutes of brisk walking a day is a real starting point, and it counts towards the 150 minutes of moderate activity a week that UK guidelines recommend. Brisk means walking fast enough that you can still talk, but not sing. If 10 minutes feels easy, build to 20, then 30. If it feels hard, start with 5. The number matters far less than the habit.

Sleep and stress matter more than people think

Poor sleep makes everything harder: hunger goes up, energy goes down, and the biscuit tin gets louder. Aim for a regular bedtime, and treat sleep as part of the plan, not a luxury. Stress works the same way — when stress is high, most people reach for quick comfort food, and that is normal. A short walk, a proper meal, or talking to someone often helps more than another strict rule.

This is not just common sense; it is physiology. Short sleep raises ghrelin, the hormone that signals hunger, and lowers leptin, the one that signals fullness. So after a bad night you are not weak — you are genuinely hungrier. Fixing sleep is often the easiest weight-loss step there is, because it makes every other step easier too.

Track the trend, not the day

Weigh yourself the same way each time — same time of day, same scale, similar clothing — and look at the average over a week or two, not a single morning. A body can move by a pound or two in a day from water or food alone, and that is normal. Many people in the UK run into one small snag here: their clinic notes and scales are in kilograms, while family and friends talk in stone and pounds, so the same weight can look like three different numbers. When that happens, a quick check on a kg to stone and pounds converter clears it up in seconds, and you can get back to the trend that actually matters.

What the numbers say in the UK

This is not a rare struggle. According to the Health Survey for England 2022/23, published by NHS England, 29% of adults in England are living with obesity, and 64% are either overweight or living with obesity. In the same year there were around 1.2 million hospital admissions where obesity was a factor. The World Health Organization reported in 2022 that 1 in 8 people worldwide are living with obesity. These are big numbers, but they carry a hopeful message too: when a problem is this common, the help around it is well tested, and small changes are proven to work.

The NHS and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) both use the same safe yardstick: losing 0.5 to 1 kg a week, aiming for 5 to 10% of your body weight, is the pace that lasts. At that pace, 10 kg takes about three to five months — slow on paper, but it is the pace that keeps the weight off, because nothing about your life had to break to achieve it.

What actually works — the evidence

The strongest proof comes from the DiRECT trial, published in The Lancet in 2018. Researchers at Newcastle University and the University of Glasgow worked with 306 people with type 2 diabetes across 49 GP practices. Half followed a structured low-calorie programme with support to keep the weight off; the others received standard care. After one year, 46% of the programme group were in remission from type 2 diabetes, compared with 4% in the control group. The average weight loss was 10 kg, and among those who lost 15 kg or more, 86% were in remission. Two years on, 36% were still in remission.

The NHS then took that research into the real world with its Type 2 Diabetes Path to Remission Programme. Its 2024 results, also published in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology, covered 7,540 people referred to the programme: among those who completed it, 32% were in remission, with an average weight loss of around 10 kg. The lesson is the same in a trial and in real clinics: significant weight loss, with proper support, can change the course of a serious condition.

Support is the part people skip. The NHS Digital Weight Management Programme and NHS Better Health exist precisely because doing this alone is harder than doing it with a plan, a check-in, and someone to ask. Diabetes UK also publishes free, practical guidance on food, activity, and weight for anyone living with or at risk of type 2 diabetes. If your GP refers you to one of these programmes, take the referral — it is free, and it works better than going it alone.

A simple first week

If you want a starting point rather than another article, here is one week that asks for almost nothing:

Day 1: Weigh yourself in the morning and write the number down, in whatever unit makes sense to you. That number is your starting line, not a judgement.

Day 2: Add one proper breakfast — eggs, oats, or yoghurt with fruit — instead of skipping it or grabbing something sugary.

Day 3: Walk briskly for 10 minutes after lunch, the Active 10 way.

Day 4: Drink water with every meal, and keep a glass on your desk.

Day 5: Cook one meal at home that you would normally order in.

Day 6: Go to bed 30 minutes earlier than usual.

Day 7: Weigh yourself again, same way as Day 1. Compare the two numbers — and then keep going for a second week.

Nothing here is dramatic, and that is the point. A week like this proves to yourself that change is possible without misery, and it gives you a real baseline to build on.

Mistakes that slow everyone down

The first mistake is skipping meals to save calories. It almost always backfires: hunger builds, willpower runs out by the evening, and the day ends with more food, not less. The second is the all-or-nothing trap — one bad meal becomes a bad week, because the plan was perfect or it was nothing. A single meal never ruins a trend; only quitting does. The third is weighing yourself five times a day and panicking at every small rise. Water, salt, and a heavy dinner can move the scale by a pound or two overnight, and none of it is fat. Judge the average over two weeks, not the number on a Tuesday morning.

The bottom line

Weight loss is not a sprint, and it is not a test of willpower. It is a set of small habits, repeated until they feel normal. Eat well most of the time, move most days, sleep properly, and judge yourself on the trend over weeks, not on one bad day. Start small, start today, and let the small wins add up.
